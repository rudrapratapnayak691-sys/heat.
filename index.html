Where Heat Comes From

Everything inside a spacecraft produces heat. Without a way to remove it, the habitat would quickly become too hot to live in.

People

Each crew member generates roughly 100 watts of metabolic heat at rest and more during exercise. That heat must be carried away by air and cooling systems.

Equipment & Electronics

Computers, lights, pumps, experiment racks, and power systems continuously produce waste heat. Cold plates and heat exchangers collect this heat from critical hardware.

Sunlight

When the habitat faces the Sun, external surfaces absorb solar energy. Multilayer insulation (MLI) blankets and reflective coatings limit how much of that heat enters the modules.

Air Circulation

Fans keep air moving so heat and humidity do not pool in one place. The moving air also carries heat to cooling heat exchangers inside the modules.

How Heat Moves Through the Habitat

The Active Thermal Control System (ATCS) collects heat inside, transports it, and rejects it to space. On the ISS this happens in two stages.

Internal Loop (Water)

Inside the pressurized modules, purified water circulates through cold plates and air-to-water heat exchangers. It picks up heat from equipment and cabin air. Water is used inside because it is non-toxic if a small leak occurs.

Interface Heat Exchanger

The warm water transfers its heat to the external ammonia loop through Interface Heat Exchangers (IFHX). This keeps the toxic ammonia entirely outside the living volume.

External Loop (Ammonia)

Anhydrous ammonia carries the heat along the truss to large radiator wings. Ammonia stays liquid in the extreme cold of space and has excellent heat-transfer properties.

Sensors & Control

Hundreds of temperature sensors monitor the system. Pumps, valves, and radiator pointing keep the cabin between roughly 18–27 °C (65–80 °F) for crew comfort.

Radiators: Dumping Heat into Space

In the vacuum of space there is no air to carry heat away. Radiators are the only way to reject large amounts of heat.

How Radiators Work

Hot ammonia flows through tubes inside large flat panels. The panels radiate infrared energy into space and the cooled ammonia returns to collect more heat. The ISS External Active Thermal Control System can reject up to about 70 kW.

Pointing & Protection

Radiator wings rotate so they face cold space rather than the Sun. In Earth’s shadow they may be oriented to avoid freezing the ammonia. Special coatings maximize infrared emission.

Overheating Protection

If a radiator or pump fails, loads can be shifted between the two independent ammonia loops. Non-critical equipment can be powered down, and heaters can protect against cold extremes.

Everyday Comfort

Temperature control is only part of making a confined habitat feel livable. Lighting, space, and quiet also matter.

Lighting

Adjustable LED lighting can shift from bright cool-white during work hours to warmer, dimmer light in the evening. This helps support circadian rhythms even with 16 sunrises a day.

Private Sleep Quarters

Each crew member has a small private compartment roughly the size of a phone booth. A sleeping bag is tethered so the person does not float away. Eye masks and earplugs help block light and noise.

Seating & Work Areas

Foot restraints, handholds, and adjustable workstations let people stay in place while working. Soft surfaces and personal items help make the modules feel more like a home.

Noise Control

Fans and pumps run constantly, creating a steady background noise. Acoustic treatments, quieter equipment, and personal ear protection reduce fatigue. Sleep periods aim for lower noise levels.

Entertainment & Staying Connected

Free time and connection to Earth help crew members stay healthy and motivated during long missions.

Movies, Music & Games

Crew members watch movies, listen to music, and play games on laptops or tablets during off-duty hours. Personal music and favorite shows provide familiar comfort.

Reading & Hobbies

Books (physical or digital), photography of Earth, and personal hobbies give quiet downtime. Many astronauts enjoy simply looking out the windows at the planet below.

Video Calls & Messages

Scheduled video calls and email let crew members stay in touch with family and friends. This connection is one of the strongest supports for mental well-being.

Shared Activities

Meals together, group exercise, and informal conversations build team cohesion. Celebrating holidays or birthdays helps mark the passage of time.

Routines, Exercise & Well-Being

Daily Exercise

Astronauts exercise about two hours every day to protect muscles and bones in microgravity. Equipment includes a treadmill (with a harness), a cycle ergometer, and a resistive exercise device (ARED).

Consistent Routines

Regular sleep and wake times, scheduled meals, and clear work/rest boundaries help maintain circadian rhythms and reduce stress even when the Sun rises every 90 minutes.

Personal Space

Having a private sleep quarter for rest and personal items gives each crew member a place to retreat. This privacy is important for long-duration missions.

Relaxation

Looking at Earth from the Cupola, listening to music, or simply floating quietly are common ways crew members unwind after a busy day of experiments and maintenance.

Current Spacecraft vs Future Habitat Concepts

Current (ISS & Near-Term)

  • Internal water loops + external ammonia radiators
  • Up to ~70 kW heat rejection capacity
  • Small private sleep quarters with sleeping bags
  • Two hours of daily exercise required
  • Adjustable lighting beginning to support circadian rhythms
  • Limited personal volume and constant background noise

Future Concepts

  • More efficient and maintainable radiator systems
  • Larger private quarters with better acoustic isolation
  • Dynamic lighting fully tuned to circadian needs
  • Improved exercise equipment with less noise and vibration
  • More flexible shared spaces for meals and recreation
  • Still relies on active cooling and intentional design for comfort

Future habitats will aim for more space, quieter environments, and smarter thermal control so crews can live and work comfortably for months or years far from Earth.
